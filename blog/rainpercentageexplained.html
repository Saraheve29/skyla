What Does a 70% Chance of Rain Actually Mean?

It's one of the most-seen numbers in any forecast, and one of the most widely misunderstood.

5 min read

Ask ten people what "70% chance of rain" means and you'll likely get ten different answers. Some think it means rain for 70% of the day. Others think it means 70% of the area will get wet. The real definition, the one meteorologists actually use, is a bit more specific than either.

The technical definition: probability of precipitation

A "Probability of Precipitation" (PoP) combines two things: how confident the forecaster is that rain will happen at all, and how much of the forecast area is expected to see it. The standard formula, simplified, is:

Confidence that rain will occur, somewhere, multiplied by the fraction of the area expected to get it

70%

could mean: 100% certain it will rain, but only across 70% of the region — or 70% certain it'll rain everywhere.

In practice, most public forecasts simplify this into a single number without showing you which combination produced it. That's the root of most of the confusion: the same 70% can describe two genuinely different weather situations.

Why it doesn't tell you how hard, or for how long

This is the part that catches most people out. The percentage says nothing about intensity or duration on its own. A 70% chance of rain could be:

  • A 10-minute downpour mid-afternoon
  • Light, persistent drizzle from morning to evening
  • A single heavy thunderstorm that passes through and clears

For that detail, you need to look at the hourly breakdown rather than the daily summary, and ideally check the rainfall amount in millimetres alongside the percentage, since that tells you about volume in a way percentage alone can't.

Why forecasts disagree on the same day

Different weather models process the same atmospheric data slightly differently, and small differences compound over time. This is especially noticeable in the UK, where the boundary between Atlantic moisture and drier continental air can sit right over the country and shift by just a few miles, changing a forecast from dry to wet for a specific town. Two forecasters using two different models can both be "right" about the broader pattern while disagreeing on your specific postcode.

Practical tip: if a forecast feels borderline (40–60%), check again closer to the time. Short-range forecasts (within about 6 hours) are meaningfully more accurate than anything issued the night before.

The takeaway

A rain percentage is a blend of confidence and coverage, not a promise about how wet you'll get or for how long. Treat anything under 30% as low-risk, anything over 70% as worth planning around, and treat the messy middle as genuinely uncertain rather than a sign the forecast is broken.
